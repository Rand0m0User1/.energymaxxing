# Submission summary (Energy sector)

**The Length of the Night** is an interactive 3D simulation of a city of about a million people, built in the spirit of PhET's science sims. You switch power plants on and off, move the city from Abu Dhabi to Helsinki to the Moon, scrub through a day and a year, and watch energy flow from each plant to each district as moving particles. A live dashboard shows whether the lights stay on, the cost per MWh actually used, carbon, and how much solar is wasted.

**Our answer, which the sim lets you discover:** cheap solar and batteries become the main source of energy almost everywhere. A firm, always-on layer (fission, geothermal, hydro, later perhaps fusion) is insurance, and how much you need depends on how long the night is. 5.2 GW of solar and 19 GWh of batteries keep a 1 GW city lit all year in Abu Dhabi. The same plants in a Helsinki December leave it dark for weeks, until you add a reactor.

**How it covers the four questions:**
1. **Energy mix, on Earth and beyond.** Fourteen places, with a real sun model and seeded weather. "Dark doldrums" weeks are included, and the city's overbuild ratio is shown against the Sepulveda et al. bands (5–8× peak without firm power, 1.3–2.6× with it). In orbit, on the Moon and on Mars, cost turns into launch mass: riding out a 350-hour lunar night takes about 1,750 kg of batteries per kW, against about 150 kg per kW for a small reactor.
2. **Fission and fusion at scale.** A five-level reactor cutaway ends in a steerable 3D chain reaction, where you hold k = 1 with control rods. Reactor Row builds ten reactors along the US, French and Korean learning paths, from the $13,400/kW Vogtle price toward $3,000/kW. A capital-stack tower shows who pays at each stage. A fusion lab shows the tokamak, pulsed and laser approaches, with a readiness bar for each.
3. **Battery learning rate and bottlenecks.** The Battery Factory doubles cumulative production along Wright's law. Pack prices fall fast; the installed-system price falls slowly. Cards cover the bottlenecks: grid connection queues, materials, permits, fire codes and transformers.
4. **What cheap energy unlocks.** The Industry Quarter has a price dial from $100 to $1/MWh. An aluminium smelter, a data center, a hydrogen and ammonia plant, and desalination or direct air capture each react at their own break-even. The lesson: industries that can store their product and run when power is cheap win the most.

**Built to be honest:** every number comes from one facts registry with a source, and model assumptions are labelled in the interface. A Guided Tour of ten short lessons asks you to predict first, then try one thing. Engine tests check the spec's benchmarks, including the Abu Dhabi round-the-clock case, capacity factors, the lunar night battery mass and energy conservation.
